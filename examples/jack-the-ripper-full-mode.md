# Jack the Ripper: a Convergence Guard Full Mode case study

[Русская версия](jack-the-ripper-full-mode.ru.md)

> Historical-analysis example, run on 2026-10-06. This is a worked example of the method, not a claim of legal guilt. The protocol deliberately distinguishes a historically important investigative pathway from a defensible personal attribution.

## Question

Who, according to the most reliable evidence available today, was most likely Jack the Ripper? Consider the major serious candidates and decide whether the evidence justifies singling out one person.

The case is well suited to Convergence Guard because it combines incomplete archives, late recollections, disputed identity mapping, retrospective suspect construction, uncertain evidential independence, and an attractive but contested forensic claim.

## Solo baseline

Before Full Mode, a conventional single-context analysis produced a provisional conclusion:

> Aaron Kosminski appeared to be the strongest named candidate, while an unknown or no-longer-identifiable local offender remained the strongest broad alternative.

That conclusion was frozen as a **solo-analysis baseline only**. It was explicitly excluded from blind search, screening, mapping, dossier, adjudication, and second-opinion inputs.

## Phase A — frozen evidence brief

### Decision contract

**Decision question:** Which identity hypothesis is best supported by the surviving evidence, and is the evidence sufficient to single out one person?

**Do not assume in advance:**

- that all canonical murders were committed by one person;
- that the offender must be among today's named suspects;
- that late police recollections correctly map to a specific modern identity;
- that disappearance, death, institutionalization, proximity, or later violence proves causation.

**Success criterion:** prefer evidence with strong provenance, temporal proximity, direct crime linkage, discriminability, and independence. Do not multiply dependent sources.

**Cost of error:** falsely converting suspicion into historical guilt is harder to undo than preserving uncertainty.

### Main factual anchors

1. **The police “Kosminski / Polish Jew” pathway.**  
   Melville Macnaghten's 1894 memorandum names Druitt, “Kosminski,” and Ostrog as suspects. Robert Anderson later wrote that a Polish-Jewish suspect had been identified by a witness who refused to testify. Donald Swanson's marginal annotations to Anderson's book supply the name “Kosminski.”  
   Sources: [Macnaghten memorandum](https://www.casebook.org/official_documents/memo/), [Anderson/Swanson discussion](https://www.casebook.org/dissertations/dst-koz/).

2. **Identity-mapping conflict.**  
   Swanson's annotation says the suspect died shortly after institutional transfer. The documented Aaron Kosminski died on 24 March 1919. This does not disprove that a police suspect called “Kosminski” existed, but it materially weakens the direct mapping from Swanson's referent to Aaron.  
   Source: [Kosminski chronology and discussion](https://www.casebook.org/dissertations/dst-koz/).

3. **The shawl mtDNA claim.**  
   A 2019 *Journal of Forensic Sciences* paper reported mitochondrial-DNA findings linked by the authors to Catherine Eddowes and the Kosminski maternal line. In 2024 the journal issued an official Expression of Concern because concerns about the mtDNA conclusions could not be resolved and the original raw data were unavailable.  
   Sources: [2019 paper](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/1556-4029.14038), [2024 Expression of Concern](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/1556-4029.15595).

4. **Druitt pathway.**  
   Macnaghten treated Montague John Druitt as a serious suspect and referred to private information suggesting family suspicion. The attractive causal story is that the murder series stopped because Druitt disappeared and died, but the family suspicion is not independently established in the frozen brief and cessation is not itself proof of causation.

5. **Tumblety pathway.**  
   Former Special Branch chief John Littlechild privately wrote in 1913 that Francis Tumblety had been a suspect and called him “a very likely” one, mentioning a Scotland Yard dossier. This establishes a genuine historical police suspect pathway, not direct crime linkage.  
   Source: [Littlechild letter](https://www.casebook.org/official_documents/lcletter/).

6. **Chapman / Kłosowski pathway.**  
   Frederick Abberline publicly considered George Chapman / Seweryn Kłosowski after Chapman's later poisoning murders, while also stating that Scotland Yard had not established the Ripper's identity. The model requires a major change in modus operandi and bridging evidence is weak.  
   Source: [Pall Mall Gazette, 24 March 1903](https://www.casebook.org/press_reports/pall_mall_gazette/19030324/).

7. **Cross / Lechmere pathway.**  
   Charles Cross was the man who reported finding Polly Nichols. The same observation is compatible with both an interrupted-offender model and an innocent-finder model; without a symmetry-breaking fact, discriminability is low.

8. **Hyam Hyams pathway.**  
   Hyams is a modern archival reconstruction based on witness-description and medical-record matching. It is useful for hypothesis generation but is vulnerable to retrospective selection and look-elsewhere effects without an 1888 police or crime-proximate link.

9. **Unknown / lost-to-history offender.**  
   The surviving named-suspect pool is not guaranteed to contain the offender. This is a mandatory outside alternative, but weakness of named suspects is not positive proof that the offender was unknown.

## Phase B — isolated causal search

Three fresh isolated search contexts received the same frozen evidence brief and decision contract, but different mandates:

1. **Institutional / police pathway:** test accurate identification, corrupted identity, retrospective hardening of suspicion, and decentralized officer-specific suspect pathways.
2. **Event / behavior pathway:** test interruption/proximity, cessation after disappearance or death, large modus-operandi change, and imperfect-canon effects.
3. **Archival / identity-resolution pathway:** test name conflation, forensic identity closure, retrospective phenotype matching, and lost-to-history selection effects.

One initial event/behavior worker failed to report back. It was **not** replaced by sequential parent reasoning. A new fresh worker was launched with the same frozen inputs and mandate and without seeing the other branches.

### Coverage gate

The gate passed without expansion. The three branches covered materially distinct causal regions:

- institutional memory and suspect identification;
- event timing, behavior, opportunity, and canon assumptions;
- archival survival, name resolution, forensic identity, and selection effects.

No fourth or fifth search worker was added merely to increase agent count.

## Phase C — reduce without anchoring

Twelve neutral candidate models were created before screening.

A fresh **contract screener** and a fresh **blind causal mapper** ran in parallel. Neither saw the other's result. The mapper identified a decision-relevant ambiguity inside the police-identification family, so a fresh boundary critic was triggered.

### Important family distinctions

- accurate witness identification + correct mapping to Aaron Kosminski;
- genuine identification event + later identity corruption;
- serious suspicion later hardened into a solved-case memory;
- decentralized officer-specific suspect pathways;
- physical / forensic identity;
- archive-selection and unknown-offender models;
- post-series trajectory models such as Druitt cessation and Chapman continuation.

The boundary audit found that “real identification but wrong modern identity” and “institutional name conflation” are nested, while “retrospective hardening” and “decentralized police suspicions” are distinct but can coexist.

### Finalist slate

The slate was not padded to three weak person-level candidates. It contained three causally distinct models:

1. **F-A — accurate police-ID chain to Aaron Kosminski.**
2. **F-B — surviving police accounts represent multiple competing suspect pathways rather than independently convergent institutional identification.**
3. **F-C — archival selection, canon uncertainty, and incomplete records leave an unknown / lost-to-history offender as a mandatory outside alternative.**

Druitt, Tumblety, Chapman, Cross, and Hyams remained relevant evidence and excluded alternatives, but none had enough direct and discriminating support to justify a finalist slot for singular personal attribution.

## Phase D — independent dossiers and adjudication

Each finalist received a fresh isolated dossier worker.

### F-A: Aaron Kosminski

The model has a potentially powerful mechanism: a witness identifies the offender, the witness refuses testimony, the result survives in police memory, Swanson supplies the surname, and the surname maps to Aaron.

Its weakness is that several load-bearing bridges must all hold:

1. the witness identification happened and was reliable;
2. Anderson and Swanson referred to the same event and person;
3. Macnaghten's and Swanson's “Kosminski” refer to the same person;
4. that person was specifically Aaron Kosminski;
5. the absence of a contemporaneous named identification record is archival loss rather than absence of the event;
6. Swanson's “died shortly afterwards” is wrong if Aaron is intended.

The same Swanson annotation that provides the identifying surname also contains the material chronology conflict. The dossier therefore classified personal attribution as fragile rather than robust.

### F-B: competing police suspect pathways

This model explains why senior officers preserved materially different suspects:

- Macnaghten: Druitt / Kosminski / Ostrog;
- Anderson / Swanson: Polish-Jew / Kosminski line;
- Littlechild: Tumblety;
- Abberline: Chapman.

It does **not** require these pathways to be fully independent. Its narrower claim is that surviving evidence does not demonstrate an independently convergent institutional identification of one person.

### F-C: archival selection and unknown offender

This model explains why named candidates can look disproportionately important: police files, asylum records, press coverage, later crimes, and surviving biographies preferentially preserve some people.

Its main danger is unfalsifiability. It cannot gain positive weight simply because named candidates are weak. It remains a constraint on over-attribution and must yield to direct, independently reproducible person-specific evidence.

### Slate-level adjudication

The fresh adjudicator performed:

- assumption sensitivity;
- shared-bias audit;
- pairwise decision collision.

The Aaron model was highly sensitive to three assumptions: accurate witness identification, same-referent Anderson→Swanson chaining, and “Kosminski”→Aaron mapping. Mutating any one materially collapsed the direct personal-attribution case.

The other two models were more robust as explanations of the **structure of the surviving evidence** and can coexist.

The shared-bias audit identified recurring proxy errors:

- suspicion → guilt;
- institutionalization → violent capability;
- proximity → identity;
- cessation → causation;
- repeated police endorsement → independent corroboration.

### Second-opinion trigger

The adjudicator triggered a fresh second opinion because:

- personal accusation is difficult to reverse historiographically and reputationally;
- named-candidate evidence remained fragile;
- key provenance and identity bridges were missing.

The second-opinion reviewer received raw evidence provenance and neutral claims, but not the adjudicator's preferred result.

Its finding was narrower than the solo baseline:

> The strongest surviving **named investigative pathway** is “Kosminski,” but the raw evidence does not justify converting that pathway into a defensible singular identity as **Aaron Kosminski**.

It also rechecked the excluded serious candidates and found none strong enough on the frozen raw evidence to reverse the decision or require finalist status for personal attribution.

## Phase E — evidence-sufficiency gate

The gate **failed for personal attribution**.

### Final Full Mode result

**INSUFFICIENT DATA TO CHOOSE one person.**

The method supports two simultaneous statements:

1. **“Kosminski” is the strongest surviving named investigative pathway.**
2. **The evidence is insufficient to say that Aaron Kosminski, or any other fully identified named person, was most likely Jack the Ripper with defensible confidence.**

The result is not that all candidates are equally plausible. The result is that the best named pathway still depends on unresolved identity bridges and source dependence.

## Minimum decision-changing observation

The highest-value obtainable observation would be an authentic contemporaneous identification or surveillance record — for example from the Seaside Home, CID, City Police, or Home Office — that:

- names or uniquely identifies the suspect;
- records the witness identification and its conditions;
- records subsequent surveillance / institutional disposition;
- allows the chronology to be checked against a specific person.

A clear Aaron match would materially strengthen the Aaron model. A different identity or a failed/non-positive identification would sharply weaken or collapse it.

## What changed relative to the solo baseline?

| Question | Solo baseline | Full Mode |
|---|---|---|
| Strongest named candidate | Aaron Kosminski | No defensible singular person |
| Strongest named historical line | Kosminski / Aaron | “Kosminski” investigative pathway |
| Police evidence | Strong argument for Kosminski | Real suspect history, but independence and convergence are not established |
| Swanson | Important confirmation of Anderson | Source-linked annotation and identity bridge; also contains a chronology conflict |
| Macnaghten | Additional confirmation | Confirms a police suspect called “Kosminski,” not necessarily independent identification evidence |
| Shawl DNA | Weak / problematic supporting evidence | Cannot close identity under the unresolved Expression of Concern |
| Unknown local offender | Broadly plausible alternative | Mandatory outside alternative, but not positively proven |
| Calibration | Tentative personal preference | Evidence insufficient for personal attribution |

The substantive change was not that another famous suspect “won.” Full Mode exposed a hidden merge in the baseline:

> **a police pathway called “Kosminski”**  
> is not the same proposition as  
> **Aaron Kosminski was Jack the Ripper**.

That separation is the main methodological result of the case.

## Runtime integrity note

This run used fresh isolated worker contexts for all stages whose value depended on blindness. An initial Phase B worker failed and was replaced by a new fresh context; failed blind work was never reconstructed sequentially in the parent conversation. Worker agreement was never counted as independent real-world evidence.
